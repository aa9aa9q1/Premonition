Premonition예감

Faint reveries, branching out in different directions, mingled with one another. Among them, the impression of the most familiar touch lingered faintly on his half-sleeping lips.

As on any other morning, the touch that woke him was wet and rough. Affection rained down insistently, as though nothing short of his sitting up would do. And this shared display of affection, which for a time had been his life, fluttered like a leaf falling in late autumn, wavered uneasily, and then vanished altogether.

He flailed his arms with the pitiable gestures of someone clinging to a drifting wreck, then opened his eyes to the alarm beating at his ears.

The moment he sat up, he straightened the bedding with crisp, orderly movements, as if nothing had happened. Hurrying toward the bathroom, he glanced at the empty food bowl on the floor.

‘…Chunhee’s at the hospital.’

As he brushed his teeth and washed his hair, beyond the patter of falling suds and the splash of water on the floor, he seemed to hear Chunhee barking and scratching, scratching at the bathroom door.

Drying his hair with a towel, he shifted his eyes from the mirror to the bathroom door.

‘If I opened it about now, she’d poke that little white face in and look me in the eye.’

Then it occurred to him that he was being far too sentimental for so early in the day. He quickly cut the thought short and went to the dressing room.

He put on the shirt with pale blue and white vertical stripes hanging on the rack, and a pair of black curved barrel jeans.

His backside, which stuck out rather more than a man’s ought to, was decently concealed by the folds in the generous legs of the trousers, their seams curving inward from the outside;

the thin, stiff cotton of the shirt gave his figure volume and shape, and his somewhat slight upper body dissolved into the bright stripes. He looked quite good, he thought, as good as men of average build, or even better.

Having gone through the same motions for seven years, he finished dressing with fluid efficiency, spent about ten seconds inspecting this and that in the full-length mirror, then ran his eyes, almost licking, from the bottom to the top of a white ten-tier shelving unit that stood about a head taller than he did.

Past the puppy pads and assorted dog supplies on the first shelf, the treats, medicines, and supplements on the second, the dog toys, leads, and walking gear on the third, his gaze arrived at the cosmetics and perfumes on the eighth. He reached for one of the four or five dusty bottles huddled together there.

One spray on his wrist and one at the nape of his neck: a smoky, spicy scent, just sharp enough to prick and stir his nose, flowed over his skin, and within seconds released a delicate fragrance of vanilla and bergamot, like a flower opening at the tip of a climbing vine.

Ready to go, he slung the black nylon bag from the rack by the front door over his shoulder and took hold of the handle. Then, without thinking, he turned halfway back and studied the doormat, with its illustration of a black-and-white spotted Dalmatian and the word ‘welcome’.

Dust had turned noticeable patches of the white areas grey. The yarn lay flattened in no particular direction, as though some large object had weighed on it for a very long time.

He turned the handle he was holding, put on his square-toed leather shoes, released the front door’s digital lock, and left the house, heading straight for the bus stop.

It was an unremarkable stop, but if anything distinguished it from any other, it was that in this rather hilly neighbourhood, all manner of owners and breeds of dog from the higher streets passed it on their way to the walking trail that led to nearby OO Park.

The electronic arrival board read ‘163 — Arriving soon’. He turned toward the crossing on the other side of the road to his left and saw a blue bus waiting at the red light.

Rising from the bus-stop seat, he glanced at the bus, checked that the pedestrian signal was still counting down through the fifties, and took his phone from his right pocket. Holding it in his left hand, he opened the app with the yellow icon and brown speech bubble.

A loading screen appeared briefly. Then, amid the assortment of text that filled the screen, he tapped the newest message at the top. There were several photographs,

(Three photographs: a Maltese wearing a red collar marked ‘Chunhee’, brown tear stains darkening the white fur around her black eyes with an uneasy air.)

and a message that seemed to report on her progress.

‘OO 24-Hour Animal Hospital: Fortunately, after several days of IV fluids, she seems to have regained some strength. On both the 16th and 17th, she began eating small amounts of the food we offered. She has not vomited over those two days and has also started sleeping a little better.

She seems to be out of immediate danger, so she should be able to go home within a week.’

The fur, clumped and flattened in places along Chunhee’s right cheek and around her eyes, seemed to make her exhaustion and illness unmistakable.

While he studied the photographs with a grave expression, the doors of the 163 bus opened before him with a hiss.

He quickly looked up and stepped aboard, took the transit card from the black wallet in his right back pocket, and tapped it against the reader.

Beep.

Most of the seats were empty. Taking the window seat of a pair just in front of the rear exit, he opened the wallet in his hand and looked at the photograph inside.

In it, Chunhee had the corners of her mouth turned up, her tongue out, as if she were smiling a little.

She wore a headband with reindeer antlers, and her glossy white coat was neatly trimmed into rounded contours all the way down to her paws.

As soon as he felt the complicated, heavy premonition that had troubled him for a month begin to press against his temples, he looked into the air and sighed. Then, as though there were no time now to examine the complete tangle inside his head, he turned his eyes to the passing view through the window on his right.

Everywhere he looked outside brought back a walk with Chunhee. Silently wishing the bus would hurry out of the neighbourhood, he moved his eyes about the view, feeling his way across it.

It was a quiet Saturday afternoon, and there were plenty of people walking dogs. Large, medium, small—all were sniffing here and there, perhaps catching the scent of the sunshine that filled the early afternoon,

walking in their various styles, keeping stately pace with their owners or bounding happily along, legs thrown extravagantly forward and back.

Clever, long-limbed Border Collies; Dachshunds with their adorable short legs; Welsh Corgis with great bowlfuls of backside; Jack Russell Terriers of overwhelming energy; Jindos, calm and intelligent companions; Toy Poodles, loved for their small, sweet faces; Pomeranians, second to none in that department; Chihuahuas trembling with rage; the time-honoured Yorkshire Terrier… and so on.

As he watched the charming dogs passing the window, one came into sharper focus than the rest: a Papillon, stepping lightly along, large ears fluttering like wings, its gaze held loftily ahead.

The fur of its ears, lighter than the deep brown of its coat and touched with gold, swayed in luxuriant waves, like vines flourishing beside morning glories in full bloom. It stole his eyes.

The generous arc of its mouth had the sweetness of a subtle smile. The way it tilted its head to check its owner’s expression lent it the elegance of an aristocrat attentive even to the servant walking beside it.

‘Pa–pi–llon. The lips curl inward and outward, clap twice, then on the third beat draw a circle like a ballerina’s rounded arms. Even the name means butterfly in French. For elegance, there really is no comparison.’

While the bus paused at a light, he burned the Papillon in question into his memory with blazing eyes. Perhaps sensing his gaze, its owner gave him an indifferent glance through the bus window,

and, as if in response, the Papillon too turned on him a look full of innocent, blameless curiosity. Then the light changed, and dog and owner crossed the road and disappeared from his sight.

The bus pulled away again.

His eyes, which for that brief moment had seemed to catch fire, hardened into coldness, as a room fills at once with darkness when a candle goes out.

Without a word, he closed the wallet lying open on his knees.

OO 24-Hour Animal Hospital was well known locally. People from the surrounding neighbourhoods came and went like regulars, and it was always crowded with owners and dogs.

His shoes clicked as he walked up to the entrance and stopped.

Among the dogs waiting for their owners beyond the hospital window, a Shih Tzu raised its head, noticed him, and put its front paws against the glass, making the most of its charm.

He closed his left eye in a small wink at the stranger’s Shih Tzu wagging its tail at him, then inspected his reflection in the glass.

Reaching behind his neck, he fussed with a slightly folded corner of his collar, rearranged the creases of the shirt tucked into his trousers until his belt was just barely visible, and smoothed down a stray lock of hair.

‘I really do look rather good today.’

Having finished checking his clothes, he glanced around. Once he was sure no one was nearby, he aimed his phone at his reflection, shifted his chin a little from side to side in search of his signature angle, and took several shots.

Then he pressed the button to the right of the entrance. With a whirr, the glass door slid open to the right.

“Welcome—”

The receptionist, who looked to be in her late thirties, greeted him. Her hair, swept neatly back and tied, gave her a tidy appearance.

“Oh, you’re ‘Chunhee’s!’… owner, aren’t you? You got our message saying she could go home?”

As though the hospital’s motto were to provide the friendliest service possible, she put particular emphasis on Chunhee’s name, leaving a few seconds’ pause on either side of it.

The lobby was rather quieter than he had expected.

“Hello. How’s my little one doing? I heard she started eating again two days ago, so I thought I’d come and see how she is first.”

He asked with a peculiar stiffness in his expression that might have been embarrassment or nervousness.

“Just a moment…”

The receptionist tapped at the keyboard, studied the screen for a while, then began to speak slowly.

“She’s much better than when she first came in. With Chunhee… the vet says nothing abnormal has shown up on the CT scans each time, so she’d be better off going home than staying here.”

She spoke as if eager to show how well she looked after every dog that came through the hospital. He felt an unpleasant pressure, and thought there was something affectedly efficient about her manner.

“Oh, right… Thank you. Could I have a quick look at her, then?”

“Of course. This way.”

She motioned toward the stairs to the second floor and set off. As she turned, she gave him a quick once-over, checking his expression with a somewhat puzzled look before climbing the stairs.

Following her along a corridor meticulously painted white, he brought the gap in his clenched right fist to his mouth, cleared his throat almost inaudibly to ready his voice, and spoke cautiously.

“By the way, how did you know straight away that I was Chunhee’s owner? I think someone else was here when I brought her in.”

The receptionist, walking ahead, slowed a little, gave a short laugh, and lifted her face toward the ceiling as though recalling something.

“Oh…! Well… one of the other staff mentioned being a little surprised, because Chunhee sounds like a name an elderly person would choose, but her owner was a young man.”

“Ah… right… Ha-ha. It’s not a name you hear every day.”

He replied looking a little embarrassed, his eyes wandering needlessly between ceiling and floor. Perhaps not wanting to go into the story behind the name, he hurried to change the subject.

“Oh, and I have to be away for a few days, so I’ve only dropped in today to see how she’s doing. I’ll pick her up in a few days.”

“Off somewhere nice, are you? Chunhee—Daddy’s here!”

As she spoke, the receptionist stopped at the third doorway from the end of the corridor, stood aside, and gestured for him to go in.

The inpatient room was long and narrow. Metal cages the colour of stainless steel stood in two tiers along the walls, sharply reflecting the overhead light.

There was room for sixteen small dogs in all. Every cage was securely enclosed in bars, with no opening left exposed.

A few steps into the room, he sniffed without thinking. Mingled with the heavy indoor air and the smell of cleaning solution was the rank smell of dog fur.

“No matter how thoroughly we wipe the cages down every time, there’s always a bit of a smell.”

Noticing him sniff, the receptionist murmured the words almost in protest, and as if to advertise her own thoroughness.

Chunhee lay in the second cage from the left on the lower tier, looking at him with half-closed eyes. No—with her eyes half shut and her body barely moving, he could not be sure whether she was looking at him at all.

Unlike the other cages, the one to Chunhee’s left had a blanket and a food bowl in it. Briefly wondering whether another dog had gone home that day, he stepped closer to inspect the listless Chunhee and asked the receptionist, not particularly curious, but as if tossing a stone into a river for something to do,

“Has the dog next door gone home?”

“Oh, actually, one of the other staff has taken that one for a walk. The owner said they’d be here by today, but we haven’t been able to get in touch, so we’re looking after it for now.”

“…How many days has it been?”

“Five. I’m sure something’s come up.”

After hearing this, he stroked the cold iron bars once or twice and looked at the white ball of fur beyond them.

Perhaps because he was unused to a Chunhee so unlike the one he knew, his brow creased involuntarily. He reached toward the bars and held out the back of his hand.

There was a slight change in Chunhee’s small black eyes as she shifted her body.

Then, from behind him, the receptionist suddenly spoke.

“If you don’t mind my asking, what perfume do you wear? We use a diffuser in the lobby, and it would be lovely to change to something like that!”

Still holding the back of his hand through the bars, he turned his head only slightly, without looking at her, and said,

“Oh… what was it called… My memory’s terrible… Ha-ha.”

Whether her owner’s arrival had revived her, or the conversation echoing through the quiet room had stirred her, Chunhee dragged her gaunt body, with its IV line attached, closer and twitched her nose.

She bobbed her muzzle toward the back of his hand a couple of times, then backed away with a rather sudden quickness, turning her head a few inches aside as she did so.

The brown tear stains beneath her eyes were darker and longer than before, like those of an abandoned dog.

He remembered the vet saying on his previous visit that there were signs of inflammation around her eyes.

‘What if those stains won’t come out?’

He stood expressionless, looking at Chunhee’s tail, drained of life, lying motionless as if dead on the towel. Then he took a plastic bag from his bag.

He took out a treat Chunhee usually loved and offered it through the bars, but she showed no interest at all.

He remembered the day he had first met Chunhee, seven years ago.

“‘Chunhee’? Who gave her that name?”

“Her previous owner was an elderly lady, so I think she said she’d named her after someone… Anyway, she had some personal circumstances and had to give her up.”

“If no one adopts her by tomorrow, she’ll have to be put to sleep. But she’s so friendly, such a lovely temperament, and she gets along with the other dogs too. We’ve given her the first combination vaccine, so once you get the remaining shots at your vet and have her groomed, she’ll be absolutely beautiful—”

“But it looks like she’s, um… avoiding my hand?”

“Oh! Do you smoke, by any chance? Chunhee seems to have a more sensitive nose than the others—”

The staff member spoke lightly and went on with the questions and instructions.

“You said you live alone, didn’t you? And this would be your first dog?”

“Yes, that’s right.”

“Chunhee doesn’t have problems with barking, accidents, or separation anxiety, so you should be fine there. And you said you’d be able to manage ongoing treatment if she became ill—”

For a moment he tried to imagine the various problems that might arise in the future, but could not quite do it.

“It’s all completely new to me, so I don’t really know what to expect, but I’ll do my best!”

“Wonderful. Lots of people find that a new canine family member gives them someone to lean on when they’re lonely or sad, a real emotional support. If you have any questions, you can always get in touch—”

Remembering how Chunhee had backed away from the back of his hand, he thought, ‘I knew.’

After a bus ride, he got off somewhere and headed beneath a sign bearing the words ‘OO Dog Grooming / Café’ in plain sans-serif lettering, with a white Poodle’s face to the left.

A little later, he and the man opposite him were talking on the café’s small outdoor terrace, enclosed by a wooden fence in a slightly different shade from the decking. They sat at the second of five tables with cheap beige plywood tops.

“You should just get another one. Didn’t you say Chunhee was on her last legs?”

Without looking at the man across from him, who was speaking while looking at his phone, he answered, preoccupied with the faint reflection of his own face in the glass.

“She’s eating again. She’s recovering. You’re basically an animal hoarder who just doesn’t know it.”

Through the translucent glass beside his friend, he could see a few people sitting indoors. There seemed to be more dogs wandering about than people.

“Hey, I take great care of my kids. I buy them handmade treats and everything. Honestly, they eat better than I do. Don’t you, Donkey?”

His friend craned his neck forward and took a long pull through the red plastic straw in his iced Americano, then answered while scratching the whiskery chin of the black-and-tan long-haired Chihuahua in his lap.

“And didn’t you once say you might have been better off getting one from a pet shop yourself—?”

His friend drew out the words in a way that could only have been meant to put him on the spot. He laughed and countered as though it did not bother him.

“Ha-ha, but I saved Chunhee when she was one day away from being put down. You only buy pedigrees. Don’t you feel sorry for the dogs in shelters? Do you know how many get put down every day in Seoul alone…”

As though bored by this line of conversation, his friend lifted his chin and cut him off just as he was about to launch into his speech.

“Oh, give it a rest. Donkey’s got ten thousand followers, and my main account’s only got a hundred, so I think I deserve plenty of sympathy myself, spending my life waiting on a dog! Look at this. Most of these dogs have more followers than I do.”

His friend pointed to a laminated sheet attached to the table, headed ‘Meet Our Resident Dogs’.

It listed their photographs, names, ages, temperaments (‘I don’t like being picked up’; ‘Please wait for me to come to you’, and so on), follower counts, and social media handles.

Among them, the number 38,000 arrested the eye, as though it had been printed larger than the rest.

“You post a selfie every day, but have you ever put up a single photo or video of Chunhee?”

He retorted, his voice rising at the end.

“I put her in my Stories sometimes, you know? And the second I turn the camera on, she somehow knows and just walks off.”

As he spoke, he checked his own social media account on his phone.

The purple ring around his profile picture showed that he had recently posted a new Story.

His feed displayed pictures of cute Papillons, listings of puppies for sale, words like ‘pet shop’; the notification telling him he had unread DMs had a little 3 sticking out of it.

“You don’t even save Chunhee’s Reels to your Highlights. You’re just like those girls who are too embarrassed to use a picture of their boyfriend. And look at you now! Getting all dressed up just to come out round the corner, worrying what everyone thinks. You’re like some teenage girl.”

Perhaps he had expected his friend to come out with something just this blunt and unguarded. He gave a short laugh, narrowed his eyes, and answered back.

“I just. Take care of myself. I don’t let myself go like you do. And my social media is for me, not Chunhee, so of course it’s mostly about me.”

Having said this, he pressed down a bit of fringe lifting above his forehead with his fingertips.

For a short while neither said anything, each looking at his own phone. Then an icon appeared on his friend’s screen announcing that he had posted a new Story.

His friend glanced across at him.

Absorbed in his screen, he was working his fingers busily. His friend tapped the Story icon.

The screen filled with a photograph of Chunhee in the reindeer-antler headband and the words ‘Get well soon’.

His friend laid his phone face down on the table and spoke to him in a quieter voice.

“Honestly, you can’t tell Chunhee apart from any other Maltese. Isn’t that what bothers you too? You only follow pretty dogs in the first place. You’ll go on about ‘the aesthetic reasons Donkey isn’t beautiful’, picking everything apart, but say a few words about Chunhee being ugly and you just clamp your mouth shut.”

“…”

“…Anyway, this time, get one from a pet shop. Don’t make a whole production out of social responsibility and moral duty and all that again. These days breeders sell seriously gorgeous puppies for social media for about two million won!”

He said nothing, merely turning his phone with its darkened screen about in his hands.

Ding. His phone lit up. Across the top of the screen appeared a notification: ‘Calendar: Sunday—Running crew; Monday—OO’s birthday; Tuesday…’

Back home, he picked up the parcel outside his front door, entered the code, and took off his shoes. Beneath his feet lay the doormat with the Dalmatian on it, its yarn splayed every which way under the weight it had borne.

He hung his bag on the hook by the door, took off his outer layer, and opened the parcel.

As he stripped away the clear packaging, a rolled-up mat unfurled, revealing a new doormat.

On it was a charming illustration of a white Maltese kissing a little child.

He put the old, worn mat into the recycling bag holder beside it and laid the new one at the entrance.

For a few seconds he stood looking at it, then took out his phone, photographed the doorway several times, and opened the shopping site’s review page.

At first he selected ‘I like the design’, then changed it to ‘Good value for money’, attached a photograph, and tapped Submit. He quickly undressed, showered, and switched on his phone again as he lay in bed.

He opened the group chat labelled ‘OO Neighbourhood Running Crew’.

Someone had posted ‘Can I bring my dog along?’ with a video of a woman in running gear running with her dog. Replies below it said ‘Absolutely—’, ‘Running with a dog! What a great idea’, and so on.

After reading the message, he imagined himself running with a Papillon, photographs of himself with a Papillon, the likes and admiring comments beneath them. Before he knew it, the images had become jumbled, and he sank into dreamless sleep.

Cheonggyecheon, in the noon sun.

A group of people had gathered on a slightly wider stretch of the walkway below the steps.

“I heard they’re changing all the marathon venues from next month. Too many complaints.”

“People are just trying to get some healthy exercise, and instead of helping… Honestly!”

As they chatted and warmed up, a few wore vests over their sleeveless tops: thin nylon, with a hint of mesh lining showing, the fabric seemingly determined to announce all by itself that it was an ultralight new material.

Among them, in a tight muscle-fit T-shirt over his carefully maintained, firm physique and an expensive pair of the latest running shoes, he was warming up diligently. He stretched one leg, then the other, but his eyes remained fixed in one place.

“Wow, I’ve never seen a Dalmatian in real life!”

“Such a sweet dog—”

The long body and legs, the spots reminiscent of a leopard, the contrast of white and black fur: it was as though the character on the mat he had thrown away the night before had sprung into life.

Watching it, he thought, ‘Suddenly I can see why that woman next door who stole dogs wanted to make a fur coat.’

The people clustered around dog and owner chatted noisily until someone called, ‘Let’s get going.’ Then they moved to their places, as though taking up formation.

He circulated among the people wiping away sweat, catching their breath, and drinking water, offering one remark after another.

“Nice work, OO.” “Your pace is improving, OO.” “I hear you finished a half-marathon, OO?”

One woman noticed his running shoes. “Are those new? How are they? I was thinking of getting a pair.” He was about to answer with a satisfied smile when she exclaimed, “Oh, I want a picture too!” and hurried away, leaving him to swallow the reply already at his throat.

Click, click.

“Oh, Allok’s not just a good runner—looks like he knows how to work a camera too!”

“Well, my two do a bit of dog modelling now and then, so the moment they see a camera, they strike a pose. Occupational hazard. Heh-heh.”

“Bring the younger one, Dallok, next time. Bring them both!”

The woman who owned the day’s undisputed star of the running crew, Allok, was stroking him gently and feeding him a treat when she suddenly looked toward him and said,

“If our organiser says it’s all right, I’ll bring both next time!”

Her gaze caught his vacant stare. As though recalled from a daydream, he answered,

“Yes, they’re very welcome—”

A few days later.

As impeccably turned out as ever, he was going through the formalities in the hospital lobby, holding Chunhee wrapped in a towel in his left arm.

The receptionist from his last visit, the one who made a show of being efficient, turned the monitor toward him to show the bill.

“That’s thirteen days in hospital, two CT scans, the tests, IV fluids, and medication. Two million seven hundred and thirty-five thousand won altogether.”

He inserted his card into the terminal.

“Stay healthy, Chunhee—” Her eyes curved into crescent moons as the receptionist cooed at Chunhee (or at him).

“Thank you!” As soon as the payment was approved, he said goodbye, left the lobby, and opened the door.

Whirr.

Outside, the sunset was staining the hospital’s glass façade red. Standing at the hospital entrance, he took out his phone with his right hand.

He tugged the folds of the towel this way and that to cover Chunhee’s tear stains a little, until he thought he could get a reasonably satisfactory picture.

The hospital glass reflected the burning sunset, Chunhee in his left arm with her tear stains plainly visible, and his own rather handsome self. He looked at the image once more.

Then, just as he was finally about to press the shutter, the Papillon he had seen before appeared gracefully at the right-hand edge of the glass, spreading its large ears like wings, as if showing them off.

He did not press the shutter. He watched the Papillon and its owner from behind until they had gone into the hospital.

Meanwhile, the sunset cast a long shadow over Chunhee in his arms, covering her whole body. Her tear stains deepened until they were black.

그는 표류하는 난파선에 매달린 듯 애처로운 몸짓으로 팔을 휘젓다가 귓전을 때리는 알람 소리에 맞춰서 눈을 떴다.

몸을 일으키자마자 언제 그랬냐는 듯 절도 있는 몸짓으로 이불을 정리했고 빠르게 화장실로 이동하던 중 비워진 채 바닥에 놓인 사료 그릇을 슬쩍 훑어봤다.

‘…춘희는 지금 병원에 있지.’

이를 닦고 머리를 감는 동안 후두둑 떨어지는 거품과 바닥에 튀기는 물소리 너머로 춘희가 짖고 또 화장실 문을 벅벅 긁는 소리가 들려오는 듯했다.

머리를 수건으로 털어 말리면서 거울을 들여다보던 눈이 화장실 문 쪽으로 옮겨갔다.

‘이 타이밍에 문을 열어주면 하얀 얼굴을 빼꼼 넣어서 내게 눈을 맞췄지.’

그런 생각을 하던 중 아침부터 지나치게 감상적이라는 생각이 들어서, 그는 생각의 꼬리를 빠르게 잘라내고 옷방으로 발걸음을 옮겼다.
